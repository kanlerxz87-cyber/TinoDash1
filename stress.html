Crash entry versus waiting

A synthetic shock, not a historical reconstruction or prediction. Compare immediate stock entry with 24–36 months in short Treasuries, followed by phased stock purchases.

Reinvestment risk sits beside the headline: maturing bills must be reinvested at unknown future yields. Waiting can reduce initial crash exposure but miss a rebound; inflation, early-sale price risk and federal tax can erode returns. Official source ↗ · Official source ↗

Change the assumptions

Starts at 100 index units, not dollars or household assets. The first year is the shock; years 2–10 use a constant recovery. Waiting assets earn the chosen annual rate and are moved in equal annual fractions over three years, after the selected wait. Annual end-of-year rebalancing; no tax, fees, inflation or interest-rate repricing. Neither outcome is a forecast.

What this does not establish

1929 and 2008 differed in timing, inflation, rates and recovery. This simplified example is not calibrated to either episode and has no historical total-return series, probability distribution, tax, maturity ladder or sequence of yearly inflation. A real analysis requires sourced monthly total returns, yields and inflation, tax and recurring costs, plus a range of entry dates. Federal Reserve history ↗ · BLS inflation ↗
