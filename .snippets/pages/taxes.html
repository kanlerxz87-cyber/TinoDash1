The complete tax picture — separated into the three taxes people confuse

Separate the three taxes. (1) Estate/transfer tax — on the right to transfer at death (federal only; CA has none). (2) Income tax on gain — post-transfer appreciation (federal LTCG + NIIT; CA ordinary rates; step-up §1014 erases pre-death gain). (3) Property tax — an annual wealth tax on real estate (Prop 13/19; not an income or estate tax). Every number below belongs to exactly one of these.

Is the estate “over the limit”? Only if gross estate (IRC §2031: both properties + cash + brokerage + retirement + business + insurance incidents + lifetime taxable gifts, IRC §2001(b)) exceeds $15,000,000 per decedent. Property A alone ($3.1M) + B ($1.8M) = $4.9M — the exclusion is headroom ≈ $10,100,000 before any tax, even single. If a spouse survives, portability doubles it. Estate tax is unlikely to be binding; the binding constraints are income tax (§1014 vs not) and property tax (Prop 19 vs not). If estate value is genuinely unclear or likely > $15M, see the Attorney & CPA Briefing Sheet — this is a must-answer question.

4) Worked example — sale in the same tax year as death (the headline scenario)

Property A, sold 2045 for FMV ≈ $7,833,550 (5% case). Basis stepped up to $7,833,550 ⇒ gain ≈ $0. Selling costs ≈ 5.5–6.0% ≈ $431,000–$470,000 (selling costs reduce amount realized). Federal gain tax = $0. NIIT = $0 (no gain). CA gain tax = $0. BHSA: $0 (taxable income gain component $0; though ordinary income like wages still counts toward the $1M threshold in that year). Santa Clara transfer tax ≈ $8,620. Total income-tax cost of the same-year sale ≈ $0. The entire decision value of “sell now vs hold” is therefore (a) property tax carried during a hold, (b) sequence-of-returns risk on the proceeds, (c) Prop 19 property-tax savings if occupying, (d) imputed-rent arbitrage, (e) §121 if held 2+ years. A “we’ll sell later when markets are better” instinct has no tax logic behind it — only market-timing logic, which this site models but does not endorse.

Read this the right way: the 1% BHSA is not a penalty on inheritance — it attaches to income, not to the transfer. It matters only (i) in the sale year if post-death gain + ordinary income > $1M (gain ≈ $0 in the same-year scenario — often no BHSA at all), (ii) in every year of large W-2/business comp, and (iii) if tax-inefficient asset location stacks rents and gains into one year. The correct response is timing and location (harvest in low-income years; keep Treasury interest CA-free; spread sales across tax years) — not avoidance. Ask the CPA to project BHSA explicitly in any multi-year plan; prior versions of this model omitted it — that was the bug fixed here.

7) Interaction effects worth one line each

  • Prop 13 vs §121 vs step-up: independent — one is an annual property tax rule (state), one an exclusion (federal income tax), one a basis rule (federal income tax). No ordering triggers among them.
  • Prop 19 vs §121: both reward occupancy but with different clocks (1 yr vs 2-of-5) and different thresholds (value allowance vs $250k/$500k gain cap) — the “live 2 years, then sell” path uses both.
  • 1031 vs ladder: a 1031 swap defers gain but forces reinvestment into like-kind real estate — incompatible with the Treasury-ladder black-swan hedge; pick one, not both.
  • Debt: mortgage/HELOC on A paid off at death (estate liability); if estate liquidity < mortgage, sale is effectively forced — ask the estate attorney about §6166/§6163 (likely N/A) or life-insurance equalization.
  • Community property: if the decedent held A as CP, both halves step up (§1014(b)(6)) — double the basis benefit; confirm deed and state.
  • Alternate valuation date (§2032): if the market crashes shortly after death, executor may elect AVD (6 months after death) — lower basis but lower estate value; only relevant if estate tax is binding (usually not) — CPA to model if relevant.
